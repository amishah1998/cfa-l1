Story

The biggest market nobody sees

Chintu's first morning at a Mumbai investment firm. Sharma ji asks where the world's biggest market is. Chintu guesses the NSE. Wrong.

The foreign exchange (FX) market is where currencies are swapped for each other. It has no building and no single exchange: banks and clients trade over the counter (OTC), by screen and phone, 24 hours a day on weekdays. In April 2025 it turned over about $9.6 trillion a day, and the US dollar was on one side of 89% of trades. BIS Triennial Survey 2025

The FX market is a global, decentralised OTC market, and the US dollar sits on one side of most trades.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market3/42
At a glance

Four jobs the FX market does

  • Trade settlement: a Pune auto-parts exporter is paid in euros but pays wages in rupees, so it sells euros.
  • Hedging: the same exporter locks today's rate for a payment due in 3 months, so a rupee rally cannot eat its margin.
  • Investment flows: a US pension fund buying Indian bonds must first buy rupees.
  • Speculation: a hedge fund buys yen because it thinks the yen will rise. Speculators also add liquidity.
FX exists to settle trade, move investment across borders, transfer risk (hedging) and take risk (speculation).

Notation, read twice

What is the 1? The CFA A/B quote

The CFA curriculum writes a quote as A/B: the number of units of A, the price currency, needed to buy one unit of B, the base currency.

USD/EUR = 1.1000 means 1.10 US dollars per 1 euro. INR/USD = 85.0000 means 85 rupees per 1 dollar.

Dealers write the same price as EUR/USD or EURUSD, base currency first: the opposite order. So never trust the slash. Ask: which currency is the 1?

In A/B, B is the 1. If A/B rises, B buys more A, so B has appreciated.

Who trades, 1 of 3

The sell side: dealer banks

The sell side is mostly large dealer banks. They quote two prices all day: a bid (the price at which they buy the base currency) and a higher offer or ask (the price at which they sell it).

What they want: to earn the bid-offer spread on huge volume, serve corporate and investor clients, and manage their own inventory of currencies. Dealers also trade with each other in the interbank market to offload positions.

Sell side = dealers who make prices and earn the spread; everyone else is on the buy side.

Picture it

Nominal versus real exchange rate

The nominal exchange rate is the quote you see on the screen: 85 rupees per dollar.

The real exchange rate adjusts that quote for the price levels in both countries. It measures how many Indian baskets of goods one US basket buys.

In the chart the rupee weakens 3% a year, from 80 to 92.7 per dollar. But Indian prices also rise 3% a year faster than US prices, so the real rate barely moves (80.2). An American tourist in Goa feels no richer.

Nominal is the screen price of money; real is the price of goods. Competitiveness depends on the real rate.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market9/42
Formula

The real exchange rate

real exchange rate (d/f) = S(d/f) × CPIf / CPId
  • S(d/f): nominal spot rate, units of domestic currency per 1 unit of foreign currency (domestic is the price currency).
  • CPIf: foreign price level. CPId: domestic price level.

Use it to judge whether a currency move changed real purchasing power. A rise in the real rate (d/f) means foreign goods got more expensive relative to domestic goods: the domestic currency has depreciated in real terms.

Real (d/f) rises if the nominal d/f rate rises, foreign prices rise, or domestic prices fall.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market10/42
Worked example

Did the rupee really get weaker?

India is domestic. Last year INR/USD = 80.0000, both CPIs 100. Now INR/USD = 84.0000, India CPI 106, US CPI 102. Find the real rate.

  1. Real now = 84.0000 × 102/106 = 80.8302 (last year 80.0000)
  2. Nominal dollar change = 84/80 - 1 = 5.00%
  3. Real dollar change = 80.8302/80 - 1 = 1.04%
Real INR/USD = 80.8302: up only 1.04%. Higher Indian inflation ate most of the nominal fall.
Always deflate: a currency that falls by about the inflation gap has not made exports cheaper.

Interpret it

Rising real rate: who gains?

Quote it as domestic per foreign, real (d/f). For India that is real INR/USD.

  • Real (d/f) rises: the rupee buys fewer US goods; Indian goods look cheap to Americans. The rupee has a real depreciation and Indian exports gain competitiveness.
  • Real (d/f) falls: real appreciation of the rupee. Tirupur T-shirts look pricey in New York; imports look cheap in Mumbai.

The trap: in d/f form, a rise is bad news for the domestic currency but good news for competitiveness.

A higher real d/f rate means a weaker domestic currency in real terms and more competitive domestic exports.

Deals, 1 of 3

Spot: swap money now

A spot transaction exchanges two currencies at today's rate for immediate delivery. "Immediate" in FX usually means T+2, two business days after the trade date. A few pairs, such as USD/CAD, settle T+1.

Used for: paying an invoice, buying foreign shares, a tourist's travel money, a speculator's quick bet.

Example: at USD/EUR = 1.1000, buying EUR 1 million costs USD 1.10 million, paid two business days later.

Spot = today's price, settled in about two business days.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market13/42
Deals, 2 of 3

Forward: lock the rate now, swap later

A forward contract fixes today the rate for an exchange on a date beyond spot settlement, for example in 3 months.

A Mumbai electronics importer owes USD 500,000 in 3 months. If the rupee falls from 85 to 90 per dollar, the bill jumps by INR 2.5 million. She buys dollars forward at 86.00 and now knows her rupee cost today: INR 43 million.

Used for: hedging future payments and receipts. Speculators use them too.

A forward removes the uncertainty of a future exchange by fixing the rate now.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market14/42
Deals, 3 of 3

FX swap: spot one way, forward back

An FX swap combines a spot deal and a forward deal in the opposite direction, agreed together.

Used for:

  • Rolling a hedge: a forward is about to mature but the exposure lasts longer, so the hedger swaps it out to a later date.
  • Funding: a bank with dollars but needing rupees for 3 months swaps them, in effect borrowing rupees against dollars.

FX swaps are the most traded FX product by value.

FX swap = spot leg plus reverse forward leg; it shifts timing, not long-run exposure.

Picture it

The regime ladder: rigid to flexible

An exchange rate regime is the set of rules a country follows for its currency's value. From most rigid to most flexible:

  1. No separate legal tender: dollarisation, monetary union
  2. Currency board
  3. Fixed parity
  4. Target zone
  5. Crawling peg
  6. Crawling band
  7. Managed float
  8. Independent float
Climbing the ladder buys monetary policy freedom and costs exchange rate stability.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market16/42
Regime 1a

Dollarisation: use someone else's money

What: a country drops its own currency and uses another, usually the US dollar. Ecuador (since 2000), El Salvador and Panama use the dollar.

Plus: imports the credibility and low inflation of the issuer; no exchange rate risk with the US.

Minus: no own monetary policy and no say in the Fed's; no lender of last resort for local banks; the issuer keeps the profit from printing money (seigniorage). Policy is set for Texas, not Quito.

Dollarisation buys credibility by giving up monetary policy completely.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market17/42
Regime 1b

Monetary union: share one currency

What: several countries adopt one common currency run by one central bank. The euro area and the ECB are the classic case.

Plus: no exchange rate risk inside the union, lower transaction costs, deeper markets. Unlike dollarisation, members get a vote on policy.

Minus: one interest rate for all. When Greece was in crisis it could neither cut its own rates nor devalue; all adjustment fell on wages, jobs and budgets.

A monetary union shares a currency and a central bank; members lose their own rate and devaluation as shock absorbers.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market18/42
Regime 2

Currency board: a peg written into law

What: a legal commitment to swap local currency for a named foreign currency at a fixed rate, with the local money base fully backed by foreign currency reserves. Hong Kong: HKD 7.80 per USD, kept inside 7.75 to 7.85.

Plus: very credible; the board still earns interest on its reserves.

Minus: money supply and interest rates simply follow the reserves, so no independent monetary policy, and little room to act as lender of last resort.

A currency board is a hard peg with 100% reserve backing and almost no monetary discretion.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market19/42
Regimes 3 and 4

Fixed parity and target zone

Fixed parity: the rate is pegged to one currency or a basket and kept within about plus or minus 1%, using intervention and interest rates. No law forces 100% reserve backing. Saudi Arabia keeps 3.75 riyals per US dollar.

Target zone: the same, with a wider band, about plus or minus 2%. Denmark's krone moves in a band against the euro.

Trade-off: some stability and a little policy room, but the parity can be changed, which invites speculative attacks if reserves look thin.

Fixed parity and target zone are pegs without a legal backstop; the band width is the difference.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market20/42
Regimes 5 and 6

Crawling peg and crawling band

Crawling peg: the peg is reset in small steps. Passive: to offset inflation so competitiveness holds. Active: pre-announced steps to pull inflation expectations down. Used by high-inflation Latin American economies in the 1980s and 1990s.

Crawling band: a band around the peg that widens over time, a planned exit from a peg towards a float. Chile and Israel used bands in the 1990s.

Trade-off: more flexibility than a fixed peg, but every step announces policy and can still be attacked.

A crawl moves the peg in steps; a widening band is a staircase out of a peg.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market21/42
Regimes 7 and 8

Managed float and independent float

Managed float: no announced target, but the central bank intervenes based on the balance of payments, inflation or jobs. The RBI says it targets no level, yet buys and sells dollars to smooth sharp rupee moves.

Independent float: the market sets the rate; monetary policy chases domestic goals such as inflation, and intervention is rare. USD, EUR, JPY, GBP.

Trade-off: full policy freedom and an automatic shock absorber, at the cost of volatility. Managed floats can drift into hidden pegs.

Floating regimes keep monetary independence and accept exchange rate volatility.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market22/42
Big picture

The impossible trinity

No country can have all three at once:

  • a fixed exchange rate
  • free movement of capital
  • independent monetary policy

Hong Kong: fixed rate plus free capital, so its rates follow the US. The US: free capital plus own policy, so the dollar floats. China has long kept capital controls to run its own policy while managing the yuan.

Every regime choice is a choice of which corner of the trinity to give up.

Trade, 1 of 4

How a weaker currency changes trade

When the rupee depreciates, Indian goods get cheaper for foreigners and foreign goods get dearer for Indians. This is the elasticities approach: the trade balance response depends on how sensitive quantities are to prices (price elasticities).

The catch: India still needs oil. If import volumes barely fall, the rupee import bill can rise, because each barrel now costs more rupees.

Depreciation helps the trade balance only if export and import quantities respond enough to the price change.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market24/42
Formula

The Marshall-Lerner condition

ωX εX + ωM (εM - 1) > 0
  • ωX: exports / (exports + imports). ωM: imports / (exports + imports).
  • εX, εM: price elasticities of demand for exports and imports, as positive numbers.

In words: a depreciation improves the trade balance if export and import demand are elastic enough. The "- 1" is there because higher import prices push import spending up unless volumes fall by more. With balanced trade it becomes εX + εM > 1.

Use Marshall-Lerner to test whether a depreciation will improve, or worsen, the trade balance.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market25/42
Worked example

Will the depreciation help?

A country exports 40 and imports 60 (billion). Case 1: εX = 0.5, εM = 0.4. Case 2: εX = 1.2, εM = 0.7. Does a depreciation improve its trade balance?

  1. Weights: ωX = 40/100 = 0.40; ωM = 60/100 = 0.60
  2. Case 1: 0.40 × 0.5 + 0.60 × (0.4 - 1) = 0.20 - 0.36 = -0.16
  3. Case 2: 0.40 × 1.2 + 0.60 × (0.7 - 1) = 0.48 - 0.18 = 0.30
Case 1: -0.16 < 0, the trade balance worsens. Case 2: 0.30 > 0, it improves.
A country already running a big deficit has a large import weight, so it needs higher elasticities for depreciation to work.

Trade, 2 of 4

The J-curve: worse before better

Right after a depreciation, the trade balance often worsens. Import orders were signed months ago at fixed volumes, and now each one costs more domestic currency. Buyers take time to switch suppliers.

Over time volumes adjust: foreigners buy more of the cheaper exports, locals buy fewer imports. Elasticities grow, Marshall-Lerner kicks in and the balance improves. Plotted against time, the path looks like a J.

Short-run elasticities are low, so the trade balance dips before it improves: the J-curve.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market27/42
Trade, 3 of 4

The absorption approach

The absorption approach looks at the whole economy. Absorption A is total domestic spending: consumption + investment + government spending. Trade balance = income Y minus absorption A. Equivalently, saving minus investment.

So a depreciation improves the trade balance only if it raises income relative to spending. With spare capacity, it can switch demand to home-made goods and lift output. At full employment output cannot rise, so spending must fall instead.

A trade deficit means a country spends more than it earns; fixing it needs income up or absorption down.

Exam trap

Trade, 4 of 4: depreciation traps

Pinky: "Let the rupee fall 10% and the deficit vanishes." Sharma ji: "Data dikhao."

  • "Depreciation always improves the trade balance." Wrong: only if Marshall-Lerner holds, and often after a J-curve dip.
  • Forgetting the weights. With a deficit, ωM > ωX. The shortcut εX + εM > 1 is only exact for balanced trade.
  • Elasticity signs. Plug elasticities in as positive numbers.
  • Absorption. If the economy is at full employment, depreciation alone mainly raises prices.
Check elasticities, timing and spare capacity before believing a depreciation will cure a deficit.

Capital, 1 of 4

Why money crosses borders

Capital moves to where it expects the best risk-adjusted return: higher interest rates, faster growth, cheap assets, diversification, or safety in a storm.

  • FDI (building a factory, buying a firm): slow, sticky.
  • Portfolio flows (shares, bonds): quick in, quick out.

A country with a current account deficit must finance it with a financial account surplus: foreigners buying its assets or lending to it. Strong inflows bid up the currency.

Current account deficits are financed by capital inflows; those inflows lift the currency while they last.

Capital, 2 of 4

Hot money and the sudden stop

Hot money is short-term portfolio money chasing yield. It arrives fast and leaves faster.

A sudden stop is an abrupt halt or reversal of inflows. The currency slumps, reserves drain, rates spike and the current account deficit must close almost overnight. In 2013 a hint that the US Fed would slow bond buying (the taper tantrum) pulled money out of emerging markets; the rupee fell past 68 per dollar, a record low then.

Hot money flatters a currency on the way in and wrecks it on the way out.

Capital, 3 of 4

Why governments use capital controls

Capital controls are limits on moving money in or out: taxes, caps, approvals or bans. Common objectives:

  • reduce volatility of domestic asset prices and the currency
  • keep a fixed exchange rate while running own monetary policy (the trinity)
  • keep domestic interest rates low
  • protect strategic industries from foreign ownership

India caps residents' outward remittances under the LRS at USD 250,000 a year and limits foreign ownership in some sectors.

Capital controls trade efficiency for stability, and are most defensible during a crisis.
Ch 7: Capital Flows and the FX Market32/42
Capital, 4 of 4

The cost of controls

Controls are not free:

  • capital goes to less productive uses, so growth can suffer
  • foreign investors demand a higher return, or stay away
  • people find ways around them (mis-invoicing trade, informal channels)
  • they can delay reforms the economy needs

Malaysia imposed outflow controls in 1998 and pegged the ringgit at 3.80 per dollar to buy time during the Asian crisis. Even the IMF now accepts temporary controls in a crisis.

Controls can buy time in a crisis but distort capital allocation if kept for long.

6 things to remember

  1. A/B = units of A per 1 B. USD/EUR 1.1000 = 1.10 dollars per euro. Dealers write EURUSD. What is the 1?
  2. Real (d/f) = S(d/f) × CPIf/CPId. Up = more competitive domestic exports.
  3. Sell side: dealer banks. Spot T+2; forward: rate now, swap later; FX swap: spot plus opposite forward.
  4. Least to most flexible: no own currency, currency board, fixed parity, target zone, crawls, managed float, free float.
  5. Marshall-Lerner: ωXεX + ωM(εM - 1) > 0. J-curve: worse, then better. Absorption: TB = income minus spending.
  6. Hot money leaves fast. Controls aim at stability, pegs, low rates, strategic firms.
