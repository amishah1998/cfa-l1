Story

Four firms, one question

Monday, Nariman Point. Chintu must rate four firms: a chai stall at Churchgate station, a Bandra cafe chain, one of two airlines on the Mumbai to Goa route, and the city's only metro operator.

Pinky: "Easy. Buy the one with the highest margin." Sharma ji: "Margin today, yes. Margin in five years? Tell me what market each one lives in. Data dikhao."

The chai stall cannot raise prices; the metro can. That difference is market structure, and it decides whether profits survive.

Market structure decides pricing power, and pricing power decides whether today's profit lasts.
Ch 1: The Firm and Market Structures3/42
Formula

Revenue and cost: the building blocks

TR = P x Q   TC = TFC + TVC
MR = ΔTR/ΔQ   MC = ΔTC/ΔQ
  • TR total revenue; P price; Q quantity.
  • TFC total fixed cost: paid even at Q = 0 (rent, licence). TVC total variable cost: rises with Q (milk, tea, gas).
  • ATC, AVC, AFC: each total divided by Q. ATC = AVC + AFC.
  • MR, MC: extra revenue and extra cost from one more unit.

Use them to find the best output (MR = MC) and to judge profit (P against ATC) and survival (P against AVC).

Every decision in this chapter is a comparison of these few numbers.

Worked example

Accounting profit, economic profit, normal profit

Pinky quits an Rs 18 lakh job to run a chai chain. Revenue Rs 60 lakh; rent, wages, milk Rs 38 lakh. She also put in Rs 20 lakh of savings that could earn 10% elsewhere.

  1. Accounting profit = 60 - 38 = 22.
  2. Implicit (opportunity) costs = salary 18 + forgone return 2 = 20.
  3. Economic profit = 22 - 20 = 2.
  4. Normal profit = the accounting profit that just covers implicit costs = 20.
Economic profit Rs 2 lakh: she beats her next best option, barely.
Zero economic profit is not failure: it means earning a normal profit, exactly the opportunity cost of the owner's resources.

MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points

MC first falls (specialisation), then rises (diminishing marginal returns: a third chaiwala at one stove gets in the way).

Averages follow the marginal like a class average follows the newest marks. While MC is below an average, it pulls the average down. Once MC is above, it pulls it up. So MC crosses AVC and ATC exactly at their minimums.

ATC sits above AVC by AFC, which shrinks as fixed cost spreads over more units. So the two curves converge.

MC passes through the bottom of AVC first, then the bottom of ATC, which is at a higher output.

Three prices, three decisions

A vada pav maker has TFC Rs 600, min ATC Rs 11.5, min AVC Rs 10. What should she do at prices of Rs 14, Rs 11 and Rs 9?

  1. Rs 14 (above min ATC): at 400 plates, TR 5,600 - TC 4,600 = profit 1,000.

Ch 1: The Firm and Market Structures10/42
Exam trap

Shutdown is not exit

  • Shutdown (short run): produce zero, but fixed costs remain. The loss equals TFC.
  • Exit (long run): leave the industry; no fixed costs remain because all costs are variable in the long run.
  • Trap: "P < ATC, so shut down." Wrong. In the short run the test is P against AVC.
  • Trap: "The firm makes zero economic profit, so it should leave." Wrong. Zero economic profit is a normal profit.
  • Breakeven is P = min ATC, not P = MC.
Short run: P < AVC means shut down. Long run: TR < TC means exit.

Long run: economies and diseconomies of scale

Short run: at least one input is fixed, usually the plant. Long run: every input can change, so the firm picks its plant size.

The LRAC curve wraps around the short-run ATC curves of every possible plant. Where LRAC falls, there are economies of scale; where it rises, diseconomies.

Minimum efficient scale (MES): the smallest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum. In competitive markets, firms are pushed to operate at or beyond it.

Economies of scale mean bigger is cheaper per unit; MES is the smallest size that gets the lowest LRAC.

Perfect competition: market sets, firm takes

Thousands of identical sellers, like the onion sellers at Lasalgaon mandi. The market (left) sets the price where industry supply meets demand.

Each firm (right) is a price taker. It can sell all it wants at P*, and nothing above it. So its demand curve is flat, and P = MR = AR.

It produces q* where P = MC. Its short-run supply curve is its MC curve above min AVC.

For a perfectly competitive firm, demand is horizontal and price equals marginal revenue.

Perfect competition: profits do not last

Short run: if price is above ATC (P1), firms earn economic profit (shaded). If price is between AVC and ATC, they make losses but keep producing.

Long run: profit invites entry. Market supply shifts right and price falls until it reaches min ATC. Losses trigger exit, which pushes price back up.

The end point: P = MR = MC = min ATC. Zero economic profit, lowest possible cost per unit, no excess capacity.

Idea

Cournot and Stackelberg: guessing the rival's output

Cournot: two firms choose quantities at the same time. Each takes the rival's output as given and picks its own best response. Equilibrium output and price land between monopoly and perfect competition. Add more firms and the result moves towards competition.

Stackelberg: the same, but one firm moves first. The leader commits to a big output; the follower then responds. The first mover wins a larger share and higher profit. Total output is higher and price lower than under Cournot.

Cournot is simultaneous and lands between monopoly and competition; Stackelberg rewards the first mover.

Cournot duopoly in numbers

Cartels: a promise everyone wants to break

Formula

MR and elasticity: MR = P(1 - 1/|E|)

MR = P x (1 - 1 / |E|)
  • MR marginal revenue; P price; |E| absolute price elasticity of demand at that point.
  • |E| > 1 (elastic): MR positive. |E| = 1: MR = 0. |E| < 1 (inelastic): MR negative.

Since MC is positive and a monopolist sets MR = MC, MR must be positive. So a profit-maximising monopolist always produces on the elastic part of demand.

Rearranged at the optimum: P = MC / (1 - 1/|E|). The less elastic the demand, the bigger the markup.

A monopolist never chooses the inelastic part of the demand curve.

Formula

Concentration ratio and HHI

CR(N) = s1 + s2 + ... + sN

Exam trap

What concentration measures miss

  • Same CR4, very different markets: four equal firms (HHI 2,500) versus one giant and three dwarfs (HHI 7,300).
  • Neither measure sees barriers to entry. A lone firm facing easy entry may price like a competitive one, because potential entrants discipline it.

  • Results depend on how the market is defined: "Mumbai coffee" or "Indian beverages".
  • Trap: "high concentration proves pricing power." It only suggests it.
Concentration is a clue to market power, not proof: always ask about entry barriers and elasticity.

The business cycle: up, down, up again

A business cycle is the swing of real GDP and other activity around its long-run trend. Cycles are recurring but irregular: no two last the same time.

  • Peak: activity tops out. Trough: it bottoms out.
  • Expansion: trough to peak. Contraction: peak to trough.

Formula

Price indexes: Laspeyres, Paasche, Fisher

L = Σ(P1 x Q0) / Σ(P0 x Q0)
P = Σ(P1 x Q1) / Σ(P0 x Q1)
F = √(L x P)
  • P0, P1: prices in the base and current period. Q0, Q1: quantities bought in each.
  • Multiply by 100 to state as an index.

Laspeyres keeps the old basket, the usual CPI method; it ignores switching to cheaper goods, so it tends to overstate inflation. Paasche uses today's basket and tends to understate. Fisher sits between.

Laspeyres: old quantities. Paasche: new quantities. Fisher: square root of their product.

Worked example

Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher in numbers

Base year: chai Rs 10 x 100 cups, samosa Rs 15 x 50. Now: chai Rs 12 x 90 cups, samosa Rs 16 x 60 (people switched to the good that got relatively cheaper).

  1. Laspeyres = (12x100 + 16x50) / (10x100 + 15x50) = 2,000 / 1,750 = 114.29.
  2. Paasche = (12x90 + 16x60) / (10x90 + 15x60) = 2,040 / 1,800 = 113.33.
  3. Fisher = √(114.29 x 113.33) = 113.81.
Laspeyres 114.29 > Fisher 113.81 > Paasche 113.33.
When buyers switch to relatively cheaper goods, Laspeyres overstates and Paasche understates.

Demand-pull versus cost-push inflation

  • Demand-pull: spending runs ahead of capacity, for example a wedding-season boom on top of easy credit. Prices and output rise; the output gap turns positive.
  • Cost-push: input costs jump, like oil or a failed monsoon. Prices rise while output falls.

Ch 2: Understanding Business Cycles28/42
Picture it

NAIRU: the speed limit for jobs

NAIRU, the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, is the lowest unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation. It is closely linked to the natural rate.

When unemployment drops below NAIRU, employers fight for scarce workers, wages outpace productivity, unit labour costs climb, and inflation tends to accelerate. Above NAIRU, slack pulls inflation down.

Analysts watch the output gap and unit labour costs as early signs. NAIRU cannot be observed directly and shifts over time.

Unemployment below NAIRU signals rising inflation pressure.

Diffusion index: how broad is the move?

A leading index has 10 components. This month 6 rose, 2 were unchanged and 2 fell. Count a rise as 1, unchanged as 0.5, a fall as 0.

Idea

The three jobs of fiscal policy

  1. Stabilise the economy by influencing aggregate demand: spend more or tax less in a slump, the reverse in a boom.
  2. Redistribute income and wealth through progressive taxes and transfer payments.
  3. Allocate resources: taxes and subsidies change what gets produced, and the state pays for public goods such as defence that markets underprovide.

Behind all three sit the big goals: steady growth, low unemployment and stable prices.

Stabilise, redistribute, allocate: three jobs, one budget.

Picture it

Fiscal stimulus shifts aggregate demand

Start at A, a recession: AD1 meets short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) to the left of the vertical long-run line (LRAS), which marks potential GDP. The shortfall is a recessionary gap.

The government spends more or cuts taxes. People and firms buy more, so AD shifts right to AD2 and output climbs to potential at B, with a somewhat higher price level.

In an overheating boom it does the reverse: spend less or tax more, shifting AD left to cool inflation.

Expansionary fiscal policy shifts AD right; contractionary fiscal policy shifts it left.

Idea

Resource allocation: tax it or pay for it

A tax makes something dearer, so people buy less of it. A subsidy makes it cheaper, so they buy more. Governments use this to steer the economy's resources: heavy taxes on cigarettes and alcohol, subsidies for rooftop solar or research.

They also pay for public goods such as defence, courts and street lights. Private firms underprovide these because they cannot make each user pay.

Picture it

Automatic stabilisers: the budget's shock absorbers

Automatic stabilisers are parts of the budget that lean against the cycle with no new decision.

  • Progressive income tax: in a slump, falling incomes drop people into lower tax brackets, so tax paid falls faster than income and spending holds up.
  • Unemployment benefits: more people claim them in a slump, fewer in a boom.

In a boom both run in reverse: tax receipts jump and benefit claims fall, cooling demand.

Automatic stabilisers soften the cycle by themselves, and they push the deficit up in slumps and down in booms.

Three ways a government spends

Idea

What makes a good tax system

  • Simplicity: easy to comply with and to enforce.
  • Efficiency: interferes as little as possible with choices about work, saving and spending.

Picture it

The multiplier, one round at a time

The government pays a contractor Rs 100 crore to build a flyover. Each extra rupee of income is taxed at 25%, and households spend 80% of what is left.

Round 1: workers earn Rs 100 crore, pay Rs 25 crore tax, and spend 80% of the Rs 75 crore left: Rs 60 crore at shops. Round 2: shopkeepers earn Rs 60 crore and spend Rs 36 crore. Each round is 0.6 times the last.

Add every round: 100 / (1 - 0.6) = Rs 250 crore of extra GDP from Rs 100 crore of spending.

Each rupee of government spending becomes someone's income, which is partly taxed, partly saved and partly spent again.
Ch 3: Fiscal Policy15/40
Formula

The fiscal multiplier

Multiplier = 1 / [1 - c(1 - t)]
Change in GDP = Multiplier x Change in G
  • c = marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of each extra unit of disposable income that is spent.
  • t = the tax rate on each extra unit of income.
  • c(1 - t) = the share of each extra unit of income that gets spent again.

A higher MPC raises the multiplier; a higher tax rate lowers it, because tax leaks out of the spending chain.

Multiplier = 1 / [1 - c(1 - t)]: more spending per rupee means a bigger multiplier, more tax means a smaller one.

Worked example

Multiplier: a spending boost in euros

A eurozone government adds EUR 20 billion of road spending. MPC = 0.8, tax rate = 25%. Find the change in GDP.

  1. Spent again per euro: c(1 - t) = 0.8 x 0.75 = 0.60
  2. Multiplier = 1 / (1 - 0.60) = 1 / 0.40 = 2.50
  3. Change in GDP = 2.50 x EUR 20 billion = EUR 50 billion
GDP rises by EUR 50 billion (multiplier 2.5).
Find c(1 - t) first, then 1 divided by one minus it, then multiply by the spending change.

Spending increase vs tax cut

MPC = 0.8, tax rate = 25%, so the multiplier is 2.5. Compare Rs 100 crore of extra spending with a Rs 100 crore tax cut.

Ch 3: Fiscal Policy19/40
Worked example

The balanced budget multiplier

The government raises spending by Rs 100 crore and pays for it with Rs 100 crore of extra taxes, so the deficit does not change. MPC = 0.8, tax rate = 25% (multiplier 2.5).

  1. Extra spending adds 2.5 x 100 = Rs 250 crore
  2. Extra tax cuts household spending by 0.8 x 100 = Rs 80 crore in round 1, so GDP falls by 2.5 x 80 = Rs 200 crore
  3. Net: 250 - 200 = Rs 50 crore
GDP rises by Rs 50 crore even though the budget balance is unchanged.
The balanced budget multiplier is positive: equal rises in spending and taxes still raise output, because taxes are partly paid out of saving.

Exam trap

Multiplier mistakes examiners love

  • Using 1 / (1 - c) when a tax rate is given. Pinky's favourite: it overstates the multiplier.
  • Plugging in t instead of (1 - t): the bracket is the share of income people keep.
  • Treating a tax cut like the same amount of spending. A tax cut starts at c x the cut.
  • Saying a balanced budget change has zero effect. It is positive.
  • Reading "marginal propensity to save" as c. If MPS = 0.2, then c = 0.8.

Worked example

The debt ratio can fall while you borrow

Debt ratio = government debt / GDP. It measures debt against the economy's ability to carry it. Debt is 800, GDP is 1,000. This year's deficit is 40 and nominal GDP grows 7%.

  1. Year 1 ratio = 800 / 1000 = 80%
  2. New debt = 800 + 40 = 840; new GDP = 1000 x 1.07 = 1,070
  3. Year 2 ratio = 840 / 1,070 = 78.5%
The ratio falls from 80% to 78.5% despite a deficit.
The debt ratio rises only when debt grows faster than nominal GDP; growth can shrink it even while the government borrows.

Why large deficits and debt worry people

  • Crowding out: heavy government borrowing pushes up interest rates and squeezes private investment.
  • Higher future taxes: debt must be serviced, and higher taxes later weaken the incentive to work and invest.

Idea

Why some economists shrug at deficits

  • Ricardian equivalence: a tax cut paid for by borrowing makes people expect higher taxes later, so they save the cut. Spending, and the economy, barely change.
  • Owed to ourselves: debt held by citizens is a transfer within the country.
  • Investment pays back: borrowing for roads or ports can raise future output and tax receipts.
  • Spare capacity: with high unemployment, deficits put idle resources to work without crowding out.

Three lags between the slump and the fix

Why the actual deficit misleads

The actual deficit moves for two reasons. Policy changes, and the cycle changes. In a slump, automatic stabilisers cut tax receipts and raise benefits, so the deficit grows with no new policy at all.

So economists split it. The cyclical deficit is the part caused by the cycle. The structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit is what the deficit would be at full employment.

In the chart, policy never changes: the structural deficit stays at 2%. The actual deficit jumps to 5% in the slump of Y2 anyway.

Judge the fiscal stance by the structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit, not by the headline deficit.

Reading the fiscal stance

Year 1: actual deficit 6.0% of GDP, of which 4.0% is cyclical. Year 2: actual deficit 5.0%, cyclical part 1.5% as the economy recovers. Pinky says the government has tightened policy. Is she right?

A rising structural deficit means expansionary policy; a falling one means contractionary, whatever the actual deficit does.

Stance traps

  • A bigger deficit in a recession may be pure automatic stabilisers. It is not proof of expansionary policy.
  • Stance is about the change in the structural deficit, not its level. A structural deficit that falls from 4% to 3% is contractionary, even though 3% is still a deficit.

Formula

The quantity equation of exchange

M x V = P x Y
%M + %V = %P + %Y (growth rates, approximately)
  • M = quantity of money. V = velocity: how many times each unit is spent on final output in a year.

On the diagram, more money pushes AD up, but long-run supply is vertical, so only prices rise.

Formula

The Fisher effect

Rnominal = Rreal + πe (+ risk premium)
  • Rnominal: the quoted interest rate.
  • Rreal: the return in purchasing power that lenders require.
  • πe: expected inflation over the life of the loan.
  • Risk premium: extra yield for uncertainty about inflation and other risks.

The Fisher effect says nominal rates move one for one with expected inflation. The sum is an approximation; the exact form is (1 + nominal) = (1 + real)(1 + inflation).

Nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation (+ a risk premium): when expected inflation rises, nominal rates rise with it.

Tools

The three levers of monetary policy

  • Open market operations: the central bank buys government bonds from banks, paying with new reserves, so banks can lend more. Selling bonds drains reserves.
  • Policy rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to banks (RBI repo rate, ECB refinancing rate, Bank of England Bank Rate; the Fed targets the federal funds rate). Banks price their own loans off it, so it anchors short-term rates. It is the main tool today.

How a rate change reaches inflation

Follow a rate hike:

  1. Market rates: banks raise loan and deposit rates.
  2. Asset prices: bonds, shares and property fall; people feel poorer and spend less.
  3. Expectations: firms and households expect slower growth and lower inflation.

Under inflation targeting, the central bank announces a number, often 2% with a band around it, and moves its policy rate to hit it, usually aiming a year or two ahead. India's target is 4% with a 2% to 6% band.

Monetary policy traps

When the levers do not grip

The liquidity trap and deflation

In a liquidity trap, interest rates are near zero and people are happy to hold any extra money. Money demand becomes flat, so adding money no longer lowers rates or lifts spending.

Deflation, a falling price level, makes it worse. People delay purchases, the real burden of debt grows, and with nominal rates stuck near zero, the real rate rises: at 0% nominal and -2% inflation, the real rate is +2%.

Japan fought this for much of the 1990s and 2000s.

Near zero rates, extra money just sits idle (a liquidity trap), and deflation pushes real rates up when the central bank cannot cut further.

Quantitative easing (QE)

When the policy rate is already near zero, a central bank can still ease. In quantitative easing, it creates reserves and uses them to buy large amounts of longer-term government bonds, and sometimes other assets such as corporate bonds.

The aim: push down long-term yields, raise asset prices, and flood banks with reserves so they lend more.

The catch: if banks and borrowers stay cautious, the reserves sit idle. Critics also warn of asset bubbles and future inflation.

QE is large-scale asset buying with new reserves, used when the policy rate cannot go lower, to cut long-term yields and support lending.

Geopolitics is the study of how geography, politics and power shape relations between countries. Elections, trade wars, sanctions and alliances change firms' costs, revenues and supply chains, and the return investors demand for holding risky assets.

Geopolitics reaches a valuation through cash flows, costs and the risk premium, so analysts must model it, not ignore it.

Idea

State actors: the ones with armies and treasuries

A political actor is anyone who shapes how countries deal with each other. State actors are those with authority over a country's national security and resources: the national government, heads of state and ministers, and security bodies such as the military and intelligence services.

Example: a government bans rice exports to keep food cheap at home. Within hours, rice importers abroad and rice-trading stocks reprice.

State actors control borders, budgets, armies and resources, so their decisions move markets directly.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics4/41
Idea

Non-state actors: no army, plenty of influence

Non-state actors take part in global politics and economics but do not control a country's army or resources.

  • NGOs and charities: a campaign against deforestation changes palm-oil rules.
  • Multinational companies: a chipmaker whose factories every government wants.
  • Influential individuals: a billionaire, a religious leader, a famous economist.
  • International organisations such as the IMF, World Bank and WTO: set up by governments, but run by their own rules and staff.
Non-state actors cannot declare war, yet they shape rules, flows of money and public opinion across borders.

Idea

Cooperate or go it alone? A cost-benefit call

A cooperative country joins agreements and shared rules with others: trade deals, defence pacts, common standards. A non-cooperative country acts alone and changes rules when it suits it.

It is a cost-benefit decision. Benefits: market access, security, cheaper inputs, a voice in writing the rules. Cost: some lost sovereignty, because every agreement limits what you may do alone.

It is not all or nothing: a country can cooperate on trade and refuse on defence.

Countries cooperate when the expected benefits beat the cost of giving up some freedom of action.

Idea

Why cooperate? Security, growth and resources

  • National security: a country with hostile neighbours or a small military seeks allies, shared intelligence and arms deals.
  • Economic growth: exporters need open markets; a deal that cuts a partner's tariffs lifts sales and jobs at home.
  • Access to resources: India imports most of its crude oil, so it values friendly suppliers and safe shipping lanes. Resource-rich countries cooperate too, to lock in reliable buyers.
What a country lacks, in safety, markets or raw materials, tells you why and with whom it cooperates.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics7/41
Idea

Why cooperate? Common rules and shared culture

Standardisation: agreeing on the same rules, regulations and processes makes cross-border business cheaper. Examples: shipping containers of standard sizes, common accounting standards (IFRS), bank capital rules (Basel), aviation safety rules. One rulebook lets a firm sell in many countries.

Cultural considerations: shared history, language, religion or a large diaspora make cooperation easier and more natural. Countries with millions of Indian-origin residents tend to have warm ties with India.

Common rules cut the cost of doing business across borders; common culture cuts the cost of trusting each other.

Idea

Soft power: winning friends without a single tank

Hard power is coercion: military force or economic pressure. Soft power is influence through attraction: culture, values, education, media, food.

How it works: people who watch your films, study at your universities, do your yoga and love your biryani build goodwill and familiarity. That goodwill makes their governments more open to your trade deals, your companies and your positions in global forums. Examples: Hollywood and US universities, K-pop and Korean dramas, Bollywood and International Yoga Day.

Soft power is influence by attraction, not force; it builds the trust that makes cooperation easier.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics9/41
Idea

Globalisation versus nationalism

Globalisation is the process of growing interaction and integration between people, firms and governments worldwide: more cross-border flows of goods, services, capital, people and ideas.

Nationalism is the promotion of a country's own economic interests, including the freedom to decide without outside influence.

It is a spectrum, not a switch. Signs of a move towards nationalism: higher tariffs, "buy local" rules, tighter visa and foreign-investment screening.

Globalisation and nationalism are two ends of one line, and countries move along it as politics changes.

Idea

Autarky and hegemony, in plain words

Autarky means self-sufficiency: the country tries to make what it needs at home, trades little and keeps the economy under tight state control. Example: North Korea. Kitchen version: the family that grows its own vegetables and never eats outside.

Hegemony: a dominant power is open to global trade and capital but does not cooperate as an equal. It uses its size to set the rules and push others along. Example: Britain in the 19th century. Kitchen version: the mall owner who trades with every shop but writes every lease alone.

Autarky shuts the door; hegemony keeps the door wide open but owns the building.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics13/41
Idea

Bilateralism, multilateralism and regionalism

Bilateralism: cooperation between two countries at a time. Example: the 2022 India and UAE trade deal.

Multilateralism: many countries cooperate under shared rules that apply to all. Example: the 160-plus members of the WTO.

Regionalism, a form of multilateralism: a group of neighbours cooperate with each other, not with outsiders. Examples: the EU, ASEAN.

Society version: bilateral is two flats sharing a car pool; multilateral is the whole society agreeing on parking rules.

Bilateral is one partner at a time; multilateral is many under one rulebook; regional is multilateral within a club.

Exam trap

Archetype traps examiners set

  • Hegemony is globalised, not nationalist. Its "non" is about cooperation, not openness.
  • Bilateralism sits on the nationalist side: it cooperates, but one partner at a time, on its own terms.
  • Regionalism is not a fifth box. It is multilateralism inside a group.
  • The axes are separate: cooperation is policy towards others; globalisation is how open the economy is.
  • Archetypes change. An election can slide a country from multilateral to bilateral.
Ask the two questions separately every time: cooperative or not, globalised or not.

Idea

The IMF: the fire brigade for currencies

The International Monetary Fund works to keep the international monetary system stable. Three jobs:

  • Surveillance: it checks every member's economy and policies, usually once a year.
  • Lending: loans to countries with balance of payments problems, usually with conditions such as cutting the budget deficit.
  • Capacity building: technical help on tax, central banking and statistics.

Example: India borrowed from the IMF in its 1991 balance of payments crisis.

IMF = short-run stability: watch members, lend when they run out of foreign currency, help them fix policy.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics16/41
Idea

The World Bank Group: the long-haul builder

The World Bank Group finances long-term development to cut poverty: roads, power, schools, health, water. It has five arms:

  • IBRD: loans to middle-income governments.
  • IDA: grants and very cheap loans to the poorest countries.
  • IFC: invests in private companies.
  • MIGA: insures foreign investors against political risk.
  • ICSID: settles disputes between investors and governments.
World Bank = long-term development projects; IMF = short-term balance of payments help.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics17/41
Idea

The WTO: the referee of world trade

The World Trade Organization, set up in 1995 to replace the GATT, runs the global rules of trade between nations. Its jobs:

  • a forum to negotiate lower trade barriers;
  • settling trade disputes between members;
  • reviewing members' trade policies.

Its core principle is non-discrimination: a tariff cut offered to one member is offered to all (most-favoured-nation), and imports are treated like home goods once they clear customs (national treatment).

WTO = rules and referee for trade between countries; it does not lend money.

The geopolitical toolbox

Countries chase their aims with three kinds of tools:

  • National security tools: armed conflict, espionage, alliances. The most extreme, especially conflict.
  • Economic tools: cooperative ones such as trade agreements, and non-cooperative ones such as tariffs, quotas and nationalisation.
  • Financial tools: currency reserves and exchange, limits on foreign investment, sanctions, access to payment systems.

Each tool can be used to cooperate with friends or to pressure rivals.

Three boxes: security, economic, financial. Know which tool sits in which box.

Idea

National security tools: conflict, spies, alliances

  • Armed conflict: the most extreme tool. It destroys buildings, factories and lives, displaces people, and breaks supply chains far beyond the war zone, so prices of energy and food can jump worldwide.
  • Espionage: secretly gathering military, political or business information, today often by cyber attack.
  • Alliances: military cooperation and mutual defence. NATO members treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all.
Conflict is the most damaging tool for markets; alliances are the cooperative face of national security.

Idea

Financial tools: reserves, currency and investment limits

Currency reserves and exchange: a country whose currency others hold as reserves, like the US with the dollar, gains influence over global finance. Countries also build reserves, offer currency swap lines to friends, or push trade in their own currency, as India does with rupee settlement.

Investment restrictions: limits on who may buy home companies or assets. Since 2020, India requires government approval for investment from countries that share a land border with it.

Whoever controls the money pipes and the purchase approvals holds real geopolitical power.

Idea

Financial tools: sanctions and payment systems

Sanctions restrict dealings with named countries, firms or people: asset freezes, bans on buying their bonds or selling them technology.

Access to payment systems: cross-border payments run through shared networks such as SWIFT, the messaging system banks use to instruct transfers. Cutting a country's banks off makes it hard for them to pay for imports or receive export earnings. Several Russian banks were removed from SWIFT in 2022.

Sanctions and payment cut-offs are non-cooperative financial tools that bite without a shot fired.

Assessing a risk: likelihood, velocity, impact

For each risk, score three things:

  • Likelihood: how probable is it? Hard for exogenous risks. Clues: are the actors cooperative, is there a history of conflict, are elections near?
  • Velocity: how fast does it hit markets? High, medium or low.
  • Size and nature of impact: how big, and is it discrete (one country, sector or firm) or broad (many markets at once)?
A risk deserves attention for its impact and speed, not only its probability.
Ch 5: Introduction to Geopolitics27/41
Picture it

Velocity: how fast the risk hits

  • High velocity: markets react within hours, typically to exogenous shocks. Volatility spikes; prices often recover as the dust settles.
  • Medium velocity: the damage arrives over weeks or months, through firms' costs and supply chains, for example sanctions that raise input prices.
  • Low velocity: slow-burn, long-term risks such as climate rules or ageing populations. Easy to ignore today, which is why they can be badly underpriced.
Fast risks hit volatility; slow risks hit long-term value, and investors tend to underweight the slow ones.

Idea

Scenario analysis and signposts

Scenario analysis builds a few plausible stories (base case, escalation, calm-down), gives each a probability, and estimates what each does to the portfolio.

Signposts are the indicators you watch to see which story is unfolding: troop movements, sanctions announcements, shipping insurance rates, polls, diplomatic visits. Think of the monsoon reaching Kerala: one signpost that tells farmers and FMCG analysts which rainfall story to believe.

Scenarios say what could happen; signposts tell you which one is happening, so you adjust before the crowd.

Idea

What geopolitical risk does to a portfolio

  • Volatility (price swings) rises, and correlations between risky assets often jump, so diversification helps less exactly when you want it.
  • Asset classes: risk-off selling of equities; buying of safe havens such as US Treasuries, gold, the Swiss franc and the yen.
  • Sectors: an oil shock can lift energy and defence stocks and hurt airlines, paint makers and importers.
  • Home bias: investors retreat to markets they know, which cuts global diversification.
Geopolitical risk shows up as higher volatility, flights to safety, sector winners and losers, and more home bias.

Exam trap

Geopolitical risk traps

  • An election is event risk, not exogenous: the date is known even if the winner is not.
  • Thematic risks are known, not unknown. They are dangerous because they creep.
  • Likelihood is not enough. A low-probability, high-impact risk can matter more than a likely small one.
  • High velocity is often short-lived. Panicked sell-offs can reverse; low-velocity risks can do more lasting damage.
  • Impact has two parts: size, and whether it is discrete or broad.
Read the wording: known date, sudden shock, or slow trend. Then score likelihood, velocity and impact separately.

Idea

Why countries trade: five gains

  • Specialisation and efficiency: resources move to what each country does relatively best.
  • Economies of scale: selling to the world, a firm produces more and its cost per unit falls.
  • Variety: consumers get goods home firms do not make.
  • Competition: imports keep home firms honest on price and quality.
  • Technology transfer: foreign firms and machines bring know-how, as in India's phone assembly.
Trade lifts output and consumption by moving resources to their most efficient use.
Ch 6: International Trade4/41
Idea

Who pays for trade

  • Job losses in import-competing industries: a toy maker undercut by cheaper imports cuts staff.
  • Inequality: gains go to exporters, their workers and consumers; losses fall on specific towns and skills, so inequality can rise.
  • Adjustment costs: moving workers and machines to new industries takes time, retraining and money. A 50-year-old weaver does not become a software tester overnight.

Economists' view: total gains exceed total losses, so winners could compensate losers, but often do not.

Gains from trade are broad and spread thin; losses are narrow and concentrated, which drives protectionism.

Idea

Absolute versus comparative advantage

Absolute advantage: a country makes a good with fewer resources, or more output per worker, than another country.

Comparative advantage: a country makes a good at a lower opportunity cost, meaning it gives up less of other goods to make it.

Sharma ji types faster than his assistant (absolute advantage in typing), yet he should still hand over the typing: every hour he types is an hour of portfolio decisions lost.

Trade patterns follow comparative advantage, not absolute advantage. Even a country worse at everything gains from trade.
Ch 6: International Trade6/41
Formula

Opportunity cost of a good

OC of X = Y given up / X gained
  • X: the good you make more of.
  • Y: the good you give up.
  • On a straight-line production possibilities frontier (all the combinations a country can make with its resources), OC of X = maximum Y / maximum X.

Use it to find comparative advantage: the country with the lower opportunity cost of a good should specialise in it. The OC of Y is always 1 / OC of X.

Compute the opportunity cost of each good in each country; the lower one marks the comparative advantage.
Ch 6: International Trade7/41
Worked example

India and Switzerland: who should make what?

With all its workers, India can make 100 crates of tea or 20 watches a day. Switzerland can make 120 tea or 60 watches. Who has which advantage?

  1. Absolute: Switzerland makes more of both (120 > 100 tea, 60 > 20 watches).
  2. OC of a watch: India 100/20 = 5 tea; Switzerland 120/60 = 2 tea.
  3. OC of a tea crate: India 20/100 = 0.2 watch; Switzerland 60/120 = 0.5 watch.
  4. Lower OC: Switzerland for watches, India for tea.
India: comparative advantage in tea. Switzerland: in watches.
Switzerland is better at both, yet India still has the comparative advantage in tea.

Worked example

Specialise, and the world gets more of both

Before trade, India makes 70 tea and 6 watches; Switzerland makes 24 tea and 48 watches. Then each specialises completely.

  1. World output before: tea 70 + 24 = 94; watches 6 + 48 = 54.
  2. India makes only tea: 100. Switzerland makes only watches: 60.
  3. World output after: 100 tea and 60 watches.
  4. Gain: +6 tea and +6 watches, from the same workers.
Specialisation adds 6 tea and 6 watches to world output.
Following comparative advantage raises world output without a single extra worker.

Worked example

Splitting the gains: the trade price

India and Switzerland agree to swap at 3 tea per watch. India ships 27 tea. Are both better off than before trade?

  1. Trade price must lie between the two OCs, 2 and 5 tea per watch. 3 does.
  2. India gets 27 / 3 = 9 watches. It keeps 100 - 27 = 73 tea.
  3. India: 73 tea, 9 watches versus 70 and 6 before.
  4. Switzerland: 27 tea, 60 - 9 = 51 watches versus 24 and 48 before.
Both gain 3 tea and 3 watches: both consume beyond what they could make alone.
Any trade price between the two countries' opportunity costs leaves both better off.

Exam trap

Comparative advantage traps

  • Absolute advantage in everything does not kill trade. Opportunity costs differ, so gains exist.
  • A country cannot have a comparative advantage in both goods (in a two-good model). Its OC of one is the inverse of the other.
  • Divide the right way: OC of watches = tea given up / watches gained. Flipping it swaps the answer.
  • The trade price must lie between the two OCs. Outside that range one country refuses.
Always compute both opportunity costs before you answer; never stop at who makes more.

Idea

Heckscher-Ohlin: who wins inside each country

Under Heckscher-Ohlin, a labour-abundant country (many workers, little capital) exports labour-intensive goods such as garments. A capital-abundant country exports capital-intensive goods such as machinery.

Trade raises demand for each country's abundant factor and lowers demand for its scarce factor. So in the labour-abundant country, wages rise and returns to capital fall. In the capital-abundant country, capital owners gain and workers in import-competing industries lose.

Heckscher-Ohlin: the abundant factor gains from trade and the scarce factor loses, so trade changes income distribution.

Idea

Quotas, VERs and the quota rent

A quota that cuts imports to the same level as a tariff raises the home price by the same amount. Consumers and producers are affected exactly as under the tariff.

The difference is area C, the quota rent: buy at the world price, sell at the higher home price. It goes to whoever holds the import licences: home importers, or the government if it auctions them.

Under a VER, foreign exporters hold the right to sell, so the rent goes abroad and the importing country loses more.

Same price effect as a tariff; the question is who keeps area C. Under a VER it leaves the country.

Idea

Export subsidies and domestic content rules

Export subsidy: exporters earn the world price plus the subsidy abroad, so they will only sell at home for that much. The home price rises. Consumers lose, producers gain, taxpayers pay the subsidy, and the nation suffers a deadweight loss. In a large country, extra exports also push the world price down, worsening its terms of trade.

Minimum domestic content: for example, a rule that 60% of a car's value be made locally. It protects suppliers but raises costs.

Export subsidies raise home prices and cost taxpayers; the country as a whole loses.

A large country's tariff: the terms-of-trade gain

A large country buys enough to move the world price. When it imposes a tariff, its imports fall, world demand falls, and the world price drops.

So foreign exporters absorb part of the tariff. The country pays less for its imports: its terms of trade improve. If that gain exceeds the deadweight loss, national welfare can even rise, at the exporters' expense. Retaliation can wipe the gain out.

Governments limit capital flows in or out to:

Formula

Why the balance of payments sums to zero

CA + KA + FA = 0
  • CA: current account balance.
  • KA: capital account balance.
  • FA: financial account balance, with net capital inflows recorded as positive.

Why zero: every transaction is double-entry. If a country imports more than it exports, it must pay by selling assets or borrowing abroad, which shows up as a financial inflow. Ignore errors and omissions. Note: the IMF's BPM6 manual records FA as net asset purchases, so there it reads CA + KA = FA.

A current account deficit must be financed by a capital plus financial account surplus of the same size.

Filling in the balance of payments

Formula

The current account and national saving

CA = (S - I) + (T - G)
  • S: private saving. I: private investment.
  • T: taxes. G: government spending; T - G is the budget balance.
  • CA: current account balance, roughly exports minus imports.

A current account deficit means the nation spends more than it saves; a budget deficit pushes it that way.

Nominal versus real exchange rate

The nominal exchange rate is the quote you see on the screen: 85 rupees per dollar.

Formula

The real exchange rate

real exchange rate (d/f) = S(d/f) × CPIf / CPId
  • S(d/f): nominal spot rate, units of domestic currency per 1 unit of foreign currency (domestic is the price currency).
  • CPIf: foreign price level. CPId: domestic price level.

Crawling peg and crawling band

Crawling peg: the peg is reset in small steps. Passive: to offset inflation so competitiveness holds. Active: pre-announced steps to pull inflation expectations down. Used by high-inflation Latin American economies in the 1980s and 1990s.

Trade-off: more flexibility than a fixed peg, but every step announces policy and can still be attacked.

A crawl moves the peg in steps; a widening band is a staircase out of a peg.

The impossible trinity

No country can have all three at once:

  • a fixed exchange rate
  • free movement of capital
  • independent monetary policy

How a weaker currency changes trade

When the rupee depreciates, Indian goods get cheaper for foreigners and foreign goods get dearer for Indians. This is the elasticities approach: the trade balance response depends on how sensitive quantities are to prices (price elasticities).

The catch: India still needs oil. If import volumes barely fall, the rupee import bill can rise, because each barrel now costs more rupees.

Depreciation helps the trade balance only if export and import quantities respond enough to the price change.

Formula

The Marshall-Lerner condition

ωX εX + ωM (εM - 1) > 0
  • ωX: exports / (exports + imports). ωM: imports / (exports + imports).
  • εX, εM: price elasticities of demand for exports and imports, as positive numbers.

In words: a depreciation improves the trade balance if export and import demand are elastic enough. The "- 1" is there because higher import prices push import spending up unless volumes fall by more. With balanced trade it becomes εX + εM > 1.

Use Marshall-Lerner to test whether a depreciation will improve, or worsen, the trade balance.

Worked example

Will the depreciation help?

A country exports 40 and imports 60 (billion). Case 1: εX = 0.5, εM = 0.4. Case 2: εX = 1.2, εM = 0.7. Does a depreciation improve its trade balance?

  1. Weights: ωX = 40/100 = 0.40; ωM = 60/100 = 0.60
  2. Case 1: 0.40 × 0.5 + 0.60 × (0.4 - 1) = 0.20 - 0.36 = -0.16
  3. Case 2: 0.40 × 1.2 + 0.60 × (0.7 - 1) = 0.48 - 0.18 = 0.30
Case 1: -0.16 < 0, the trade balance worsens. Case 2: 0.30 > 0, it improves.
A country already running a big deficit has a large import weight, so it needs higher elasticities for depreciation to work.

The J-curve: worse before better

Right after a depreciation, the trade balance often worsens. Import orders were signed months ago at fixed volumes, and now each one costs more domestic currency. Buyers take time to switch suppliers.

Over time volumes adjust: foreigners buy more of the cheaper exports, locals buy fewer imports. Elasticities grow, Marshall-Lerner kicks in and the balance improves. Plotted against time, the path looks like a J.

Short-run elasticities are low, so the trade balance dips before it improves: the J-curve.

The absorption approach

The absorption approach looks at the whole economy. Absorption A is total domestic spending: consumption + investment + government spending. Trade balance = income Y minus absorption A. Equivalently, saving minus investment.

So a depreciation improves the trade balance only if it raises income relative to spending. With spare capacity, it can switch demand to home-made goods and lift output. At full employment output cannot rise, so spending must fall instead.

A trade deficit means a country spends more than it earns; fixing it needs income up or absorption down.

Trade, 4 of 4: depreciation traps

Pinky: "Let the rupee fall 10% and the deficit vanishes." Sharma ji: "Data dikhao."

Check elasticities, timing and spare capacity before believing a depreciation will cure a deficit.

Why money crosses borders

Capital moves to where it expects the best risk-adjusted return: higher interest rates, faster growth, cheap assets, diversification, or safety in a storm.

  • FDI (building a factory, buying a firm): slow, sticky.
  • Portfolio flows (shares, bonds): quick in, quick out.

A country with a current account deficit must finance it with a financial account surplus: foreigners buying its assets or lending to it. Strong inflows bid up the currency.

Current account deficits are financed by capital inflows; those inflows lift the currency while they last.

Hot money and the sudden stop

Hot money is short-term portfolio money chasing yield. It arrives fast and leaves faster.

A sudden stop is an abrupt halt or reversal of inflows. The currency slumps, reserves drain, rates spike and the current account deficit must close almost overnight. In 2013 a hint that the US Fed would slow bond buying (the taper tantrum) pulled money out of emerging markets; the rupee fell past 68 per dollar, a record low then.

Hot money flatters a currency on the way in and wrecks it on the way out.

Why governments use capital controls

Capital controls are limits on moving money in or out: taxes, caps, approvals or bans. Common objectives:

  • reduce volatility of domestic asset prices and the currency
  • keep a fixed exchange rate while running own monetary policy (the trinity)
  • keep domestic interest rates low
  • protect strategic industries from foreign ownership

India caps residents' outward remittances under the LRS at USD 250,000 a year and limits foreign ownership in some sectors.

Capital controls trade efficiency for stability, and are most defensible during a crisis.

Capital, 4 of 4

The cost of controls

Controls are not free:

  • capital goes to less productive uses, so growth can suffer
  • foreign investors demand a higher return, or stay away
  • people find ways around them (mis-invoicing trade, informal channels)
  • they can delay reforms the economy needs

Malaysia imposed outflow controls in 1998 and pegged the ringgit at 3.80 per dollar to buy time during the Asian crisis. Even the IMF now accepts temporary controls in a crisis.

Controls can buy time in a crisis but distort capital allocation if kept for long.

Notation

Price currency and base currency

The CFA curriculum writes A/B = the number of units of A (price currency) needed to buy one unit of B (base currency).

  • USD/EUR = 1.1000: 1.10 US dollars per 1 euro.
  • INR/USD = 85.0000: 85 rupees per 1 dollar.
  • JPY/USD = 150.0000: 150 yen per 1 dollar.

Read it like a price tag: the base currency is the thing being bought, the price currency is the money paid.

In A/B, B is the 1 (base) and A is the price; a higher A/B means B is dearer.

Notation

Direct and indirect quotes

Direct and indirect depend on whose point of view you take.

  • Direct quote: the domestic currency is the price currency. For an Indian, INR/USD = 85.0000 (rupees per dollar).
  • Indirect quote: the domestic currency is the base currency. For an Indian, USD/INR = 0.0118 (dollars per rupee).

The same INR/USD quote is direct in Mumbai and indirect in New York. A direct quote reads like a shop price: so many rupees for one dollar.

Direct = domestic currency is the price currency; indirect = domestic currency is the base.

Flip a quote

Notation

Bid and offer: the dealer always wins

Dealers quote two prices for the base currency: USD/EUR 1.0998 / 1.1002.

  • Bid 1.0998: the dealer buys euros at this price. You sell to it here.
  • Offer 1.1002: the dealer sells euros here. You buy here.

The client always gets the worse side. Inverting a two-way quote swaps the sides: EUR/USD bid = 1/1.1002 = 0.9089, offer = 1/1.0998 = 0.9093.

You buy the base currency at the offer and sell it at the bid; offer is always the higher number.

The currency see-saw

Two currencies sit on a see-saw. If USD/EUR rises from 1.1000 to 1.1550, one euro buys more dollars:

  • the base currency (EUR) has appreciated
  • the price currency (USD) has depreciated

If the quote falls, flip the see-saw: base down, price up. The quote is the price of the base, so the base moves the same way as the number.

A/B up means B up and A down; A/B down means B down and A up.

Formula

Percentage change in each currency

Base B: S1/S0 - 1
Price A: S0/S1 - 1

Use it whenever a question asks how much a currency appreciated or depreciated. First decide if that currency is the base or the price.

INR/USD rises from 83.0000 to 87.1500. The dollar rose 5.00%. Did the rupee fall 5%? No. It fell 83/87.15 - 1 = -4.76%.

Cross rates: the triangle

A cross rate is the rate between two currencies worked out through a third, usually the US dollar.

Treat quotes like fractions and cancel the middle currency:

INR/USD × USD/EUR = INR/EUR

If the middle currency does not cancel, invert one quote first. Then check that what is left is exactly the pair you want, in the order you want.

Line up the quotes so the common currency cancels, then multiply.

A spot deal settles about two business days after it is agreed (T+2).

Forwards

Forward points

Dealers rarely quote the full forward rate. They quote forward points: the forward minus the spot, in pips.

  • Most pairs quote to 4 decimals, so 1 point = 0.0001: divide points by 10,000.
  • Yen pairs quote to 2 decimals, so 1 point = 0.01: divide by 100.

Positive points: forward above spot, the base currency trades at a forward premium. Negative points: a forward discount.

Scale the points to the quote's last decimal, then add them to spot, keeping the sign.

Forwards

Forward premium or discount

Premium and discount always describe the base currency (the 1) in the quote.

  • F > S: base at a forward premium. USD/EUR spot 1.1000, forward 1.1045: the euro is at a premium of 1.1045/1.1000 - 1 = 0.409%.
  • F < S: base at a forward discount.

If the euro is at a premium against the dollar, the dollar is at a discount against the euro.

Forward premium or discount = F/S - 1, and it belongs to the base currency.

Formula

Covered interest rate parity

F(A/B) = S(A/B) × (1 + iA × Actual/360) / (1 + iB × Actual/360)
  • S, F: spot and forward rates, A per 1 B.
  • iA: annual interest rate on the price currency A. iB: on the base currency B.
  • Actual/360: days to settlement / 360, so 90 days = 0.25.

Use it to price any forward. The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount: its extra interest is paid back through a worse forward rate.

Price currency's rate on top, base currency's rate on the bottom, both scaled by days/360.

CIP: a 3-month USD/EUR forward

USD/EUR spot = 1.1000. USD rate 4.00%, EUR rate 2.00% a year. Find the 90-day forward.

  1. A = USD (price, 4%), B = EUR (base, 2%); t = 90/360 = 0.25
  2. Top: 1 + 0.04 × 0.25 = 1.0100. Bottom: 1 + 0.02 × 0.25 = 1.0050

Why it holds

Two roads, same destination

Chintu has INR 100 for a year (rates and spot from the 1-year card).

  • Road 1: rupee deposit at 6.50%: INR 106.50.
  • Road 2: buy dollars at 85: USD 1.1765. Earn 4%: USD 1.2235. Sell them today at the forward 87.0433: INR 106.50.

Both roads are riskless and end in the same place. That is covered interest rate parity: the forward rate exactly cancels the interest gap. No free lunch.

Covered interest parity says a hedged foreign deposit earns the same as a home deposit.

The approximate forward premium

The forward premium on the base currency B is roughly the interest rate gap, price currency minus base currency, scaled for time:

(F - S)/S ≈ (iA - iB) × Actual/360

  • 1-year INR/USD (6.5% vs 4%): approx 2.50%; exact 2.5%/1.04 = 2.404%.
Base currency premium ≈ price currency rate minus base currency rate, times days/360.

Exam trap

Forward and parity traps

  • Rates upside down. The price currency's rate goes on top. Swap them and the premium flips sign.
  • Annual rates for short periods. Use 4% × 90/360, not 4%.
  • "Higher rate means stronger forward." Wrong: the higher-rate currency trades at a forward discount.
  • Forward as forecast. CIP is an arbitrage price, not a prediction of the future spot rate.
  • Points scale. Divide by 100 for yen pairs.
Price rate on top, days over 360, and the high-yield currency is at a discount.

Exam-style, part 2: the 180-day forward

Continue: INR/EUR spot 93.5000. Find the 180-day forward and the hedged rupee cost of EUR 200,000.

6 things to remember

  1. A/B = units of A (price) per 1 B (base). USD/EUR 1.1000 = 1.10 dollars per euro. Dealers write EURUSD.
  2. Direct quote: domestic currency is the price currency. Flip a quote with 1/S.
  3. Base changes by S1/S0 - 1; price by S0/S1 - 1. Not equal and opposite.
  4. Cross rates: cancel the middle currency; invert first if it does not cancel.
  5. Points / 10,000 (yen pairs / 100). F > S: base at a forward premium.
  6. F = S × (1 + iAt)/(1 + iBt), t = days/360. Higher-rate currency at a forward discount.
